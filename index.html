Earth Systems Science, Unit 3.3 Lab

Correlating the Llano Seco Basin

Your assignment

You’ve been hired to map the rocks of the Llano Seco Basin. The basin is flat and covered with soil, so the rock layers only show up in four places: a ridge, a creek bank, a quarry, and a river bluff. They’re miles apart, and no single one shows the whole story.

Your job is to do what geologists did in the 1800s: match the layers between sites, piece them into one complete sequence, and then figure out which parts of the geologic time scale this basin records.

The Llano Seco Basin and its rocks are made up for this lab, but the fossils are real index fossils.

How the lab works

  1. Correlate. Connect layers that formed at the same time across neighboring sites.
  2. Composite. Stack every unit into one complete column, oldest at the bottom.
  3. Name it. Use a fossil range chart and two dated ash beds to label your column with periods and ages.
  4. Lab report. Your score updates as you go. When you finish, save it as a PDF and turn it in.

Each section unlocks when you finish the one before it. You can retry anything, but first-try answers earn the most points.

Investigation 3: Put names and numbers on it

Your composite column puts the basin’s history in order. Now you’ll connect it to the global geologic time scale, the same way geologists do: fossils tell you the period, and dated volcanic ash tells you the numbers.

Part A: Read the range chart

A range chart shows when each fossil species lived. A bar means the species existed during those periods. Use it to identify the period of each fossil zone in your column.
